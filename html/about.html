The Story

Where I started, what I decided, and where I am going.

I finished high school in November 2025. Most of my peers left with one plan: find a job, earn money, build stability. That is a rational plan. I understood it, and I respected it. But it was not my plan.

I left school with a different kind of urgency. Not urgency about money — urgency about becoming. I had watched people spend their twenties waiting for the right time to do the things they actually wanted to do. The right time never came. I decided early that I was not going to wait.

I had a window of time that most people do not get — a window before the full weight of survival lands on you. I made a decision to use that window to build the most important thing: myself.

In December 2025, I tried sales. Field work, door-to-door, results-based pay. I was terrible at it. I quit after one month and treated it as feedback, not failure. It told me something: I was better suited to building things than selling them. That month pointed me in a direction.

In January 2026, two things happened simultaneously. I started training boxing at Ongata Rongai Boxing Club, and I started learning how to code. I had studied computer science in high school, but formal education had never given me the feeling of actually building something. I wanted to build.

I started with a virtual assistant course in January — one month, foundational remote work skills. Then I pivoted into web development. HTML in February. CSS. JavaScript. Full-stack development within two months. By April 2026, I had five live, deployed tools on the internet. Built from scratch, independently, with no team and no bootcamp.

Alongside the building, I started writing. I launched two Substack publications in February 2026: Deliberately Becoming — on discipline, systems, and the search for meaning — and Code & System — on what I build, how I build it, and what breaks along the way. Writing is not a hobby for me. It is how I think. It forces clarity. Every article is a commitment to understand something well enough to explain it.

I also read. Not to collect books, but to apply them. Every book I finish leaves something that changes how I act — not how I think in theory, but what I actually do the next day. The books I read are recorded on the Progress page, along with what I took from them and how I applied it.

This portfolio is the record of all of it. Not just the wins. The failed interview, the difficult months, the projects I abandoned and the ones I shipped. The whole arc.

I am not trying to look impressive. I am trying to document accurately. There is a version of this portfolio I could build that only shows the highlights — the five live tools, the articles, the progress. That version would be a lie by omission. The real story includes the sales job I quit, the marathon training runs that hurt, the evenings when the code did not work and I did not know why.

That is what this portfolio is. A record that is honest, detailed, and permanent. Something I can open in ten years and see the full arc of who I was becoming and how I got there. Something that shows not just what I built, but who I was while building it.

The goal is not to arrive somewhere. The goal is to keep moving and document every step.

What I Am Doing

The Four Pillars

Four areas I am developing simultaneously. Each one feeds the others.

Boxing

Boxing is the discipline that coding cannot teach me. When I am in the ring, there is no algorithm to fall back on. There is only what I have trained, and whether I have trained it enough. The sport teaches me that preparation is real — that what you put in before the moment determines what you can do in the moment. It also teaches me to absorb failure without stopping. Every session I get hit, I learn. Every session I get tired, I learn. The ring is the most honest feedback loop I have ever been in.

Building

I build tools because I believe that real understanding only comes from making things. I can read documentation for a week and understand very little. I can spend one day building something broken and understand everything. Every project I ship teaches me something I could not have learned any other way. My philosophy on software is simple: build things that solve real problems, ship them before they are perfect, and iterate based on real feedback. The goal is never the perfect tool — it is the working one.

Reading

I read because I am trying to stand on the shoulders of people who thought about things more deeply than I have time to think about from scratch. Every book I finish is a conversation with someone who spent years on a single idea. I look for books that change how I act, not just how I feel. The measure of a book for me is not whether I found it interesting — it is whether I did something differently the week after I read it. Reading, building, and boxing are all the same discipline applied to different materials.

Writing

I write publicly because privacy creates no accountability. When I write on Substack, I am making a commitment. I am saying: this is what I think, this is what I did, this is what happened. I cannot revise history when the history is public. Writing forces me to understand my own experience clearly enough to explain it to someone else. What I cannot explain, I do not understand. The two publications I run — Deliberately Becoming and Code & System — represent the two sides of what I am doing: becoming someone and building things.
